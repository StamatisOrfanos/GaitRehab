\section{Experimental Results}
\label{sec:experimental_resuts}

\subsection{Model Accuracy Metrics}
Starting with the Fig.~\ref{fig:metrics}, the bar plot shows the classification accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score and AUC of each model 
using the selected features: minimum and maximum values of the z-axis angular velocity from the left and right leg. The models tested include Logistic 
Regression, Random Forest, Support Vector Machines (SVM) with linear, polynomial, and RBF kernels, XGBoost, and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN). 

\begin{figure*}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{Images/selected_features.png}
    \caption{Classification metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, F1).}
    \label{fig:metrics}
\end{figure*}

The highest F1-score (93\%) was achieved by \textit{Random Forest}, followed closely by the \textit{Logistic Regression} and \textit{XGBoost} models with 
accuracies of (89\%) of (90\%) respectively. In contrast the \textit{SVM} models had a lower F1-score of (85\%) for the linear and RBF kernels, while the 
\textit{SVM} with polynomial kernel achieved a significantly lower score of (78\%). Lastly the \textit{KNN} model performed  an F1-score of (76\%), which is 
th lowest among the tested models. The detailed performance metrics for each models are summarized in the Table~\ref{tab:metrics}.

    \midrule
    Logistic Regression     & 0.90 & 1.00 & 0.81 & 0.89 \\

An important consideration when evaluating these models lies in the balance between precision and recall. Several classifiers, such as SVM variants and KNN, 
demonstrate very high precision (100\%), indicating that when they predict an abnormal gait, they are almost always correct. However, their recall scores 
range between (56\%) and (75\%), suggesting that they fail to identify a significant portion of actual abnormal cases. 

In the current context of gait classification for stroke rehabilitation, missing a true abnormal gait can be more detrimental than issuing a false alarm. 
A high recall is thus essential for early detection and timely intervention. In contrast, models like Random Forest and XGBoost achieve both high precision 
and high recall, offering a better balance by correctly identifying most abnormal gaits while minimizing false positives. This makes them particularly suited 
for deployment in clinical screening settings where sensitivity is critical to patient safety.

\subsection{ROC Curve Analysis}
The ROC curves in Fig.~\ref{fig:roc} provide a clearer view of each model’s ability to distinguish between classes. While the \textit{SVM with RBF Kernel} achieved 
a perfect AUC of (1.00), suggesting ideal separation between normal and abnormal gait cases across all thresholds—this performance is not fully reflected in its 
recall and F1-score, which remain moderate (around 75–85\%). This discrepancy indicates that, despite the model’s strong theoretical ranking ability, its 
performance at the selected decision threshold still leads to missed positive cases. In other words, the model excels at ranking instances correctly but fails 
to convert that strength into high recall due to threshold misalignment.

\begin{figure*}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{Images/selected_features_models_roc_curves.png}
    \caption{ROC curves for all classifiers using LOOCV.}
    \label{fig:roc}
\end{figure*}

\textit{Random Forest} and \textit{XGBoost} also demonstrate excellent AUCs (0.98 and 0.97 respectively), confirming their strong balance between sensitivity 
and specificity. Interestingly, \textit{Logistic Regression}, despite being a linear model, achieved a solid AUC of 0.92, showing that the selected features are 
linearly separable to a great extent. In contrast, although the \textit{SVM Polynomial Kernel} model exhibited a relatively high AUC of 0.90, its 
lower F1-score and higher misclassification rate suggest a potential overfitting or misalignment between model complexity and the dataset size. 


\subsection{Confusion Matrix Evaluation}
Fig.~\ref{fig:confusion_matrices} illustrates the confusion matrices for each classifier. Most models performed well on both classes, showing strong 
diagonal dominance. Notably, \textit{Logistic Regression}, and \textit{XGBoost} \textit{Random Forest}, where each misclassified mostly one instance, 
yielding very low false positive and false negative rates. The Support Vector Machine (SVM) variants showed moderate performance, with the RBF and Linear kernels 
achieving better classification than the Polynomial kernel. These models had a mild bias toward classifying abnormal gait as normal, resulting in a higher 
false-negative rate, a crucial consideration in clinical settings where missing pathological gait is more consequential than over-detection.
Lastly the \textit{KNN} model had the highest number of misclassifications, with a notable number of false positives and false negatives, indicating that it 
may not be as robust as the other models for this task. Overall, these confusion matrices reinforce the findings and emphasize the consistency and clinical 
usability of ensemble-based classifiers like Random Forest and XGBoost for reliable gait classification using minimal yet meaningful features.
