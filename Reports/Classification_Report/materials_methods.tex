\section{Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:materials_and_methods}
This study builds upon data previously collected in the work of Sanghan et al.~\cite{ref35}, which involved a comprehensive investigation comparing the 3D 
shank kinematics of stroke patients to healthy individuals. Ethical approval for the current study was granted under protocol REC. 68-342-38-2.

\subsection{Data Source}
The original experiment included sixteen stroke patients and sixteen healthy individuals, all of whom walked on a flat 10-meter indoor walkway while wearing IMU sensors 
(MBIENTLAB Inc., USA) placed bilaterally on their shanks as seen on Figure \ref{fig:sensor_placement}. Data acquisition protocols, participant criteria, and ethical 
approvals were thoroughly detailed in the original publication~\cite{ref35}. The recorded data consisted of tri-axial gyroscope signals sampled at 100 Hz, with angular 
velocities measured along the sagittal (x), frontal (y), and transverse (z) planes. Detailed demographic and clinical assessment information (e.g., age distribution, 
stroke severity scores such as Fugl–Meyer or NIHSS) was not available in the dataset used for the present analysis and is fully documented in the original data 
collection study by Sanghan et al.~\cite{ref35}. The present work focuses on signal-level gait classification using the available kinematic data.


\subsection{Feature Extraction}
In the process of extracting features from the gyroscope data, that were located on both shanks of the participants we focused on the z-axis angular velocity 
as it it is the most relevant to the gait cycle, since the z-axis gyroscope signals correspond to leg rotation, highly indicative of gait abnormalities.
IMU placement on the lateral shank and the use of z-axis angular velocity for gait phase identification are consistent with previous methods that demonstrated high 
accuracy in segmenting gait cycles and estimating spatio-temporal parameters~\cite{ref36},\cite{ref37}.Furthermore we decided to use the data derived from the shanks 
only, due to the fact that data from other points of the lower or upper body did not provide any additional information for the task, which is also supported by the 
research of Hwang et al.~\cite{ref38}, who got the best results from the shank-mounted IMUs for accurate gait classification in joint impairment detection tasks.

To ensure a detailed representation of gait dynamics, we extracted a set of temporal, motion-based, asymmetry, and statistical features from the IMU data. These features, 
detailed in Table~\ref{table:features}, include all major categories commonly used in gait analysis, effectively exhausting the relevant metrics for the classification task. 
It is important to note that before extracting the features, we applied a low-pass Butterworth filter with a cutoff frequency of 10Hz to the gyroscope data to remove 
high-frequency noise and ensure that the features reflect the gait patterns accurately.

Although inertial measurement units provide both accelerometer and gyroscope signals, this study intentionally focuses on gyroscope-derived features. Prior work
has demonstrated that shank angular velocity, particularly along the z-axis, is highly informative for gait phase characterization and asymmetry detection
\cite{ref36,ref38}. Limiting the feature space to gyroscopic features reduces sensitivity to sensor misalignment, gravitational artifacts, and placement
variability, while also minimizing computational overhead. A comparative analysis between gyroscope-only and combined gyroscope–accelerometer configurations is
planned as part of an extended study.


\subsection{Feature Selection}
The feature selection process was conducted to identify the most relevant features for the classification task. The classification task in this case is a binary 
classification problem that enables us to use methods like Point Bi-serial Feature Selection (BFS) and Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) to select the most
informative features. BFS evaluates the correlation between each feature and the target variable, selecting those with the highest correlation scores. 
The results of the BFS analysis are shown below in Table~\ref{tab:bfs_results}.

It should be noted that BFS was used as a feature ranking mechanism rather than a strict statistical significance filter. Given the subject-level aggregation 
and limited sample size (N=32), p-values were interpreted cautiously and used in combination with RFE and clinical interpretability criteria.

Following the BFS analysis, we applied RFE with the use of Random Forest Classifier to refine the feature set further. In this process we 
iteratively removed the least important features based on the contribution to the accuracy of the model, until we got the optimal subset of features.

\subsection{Classification Models}
Although the total number of participants is 32, providing a relatively big dataset for a clinical study, it is still considered to be a small sample size. The small 
dataset size along with the modest dimensionality of the feature space, guided us to use classical machine learning models rather than deep learning 
approaches. Deep learning models typically require large volumes of training data to generalize effectively and are more prone to overfitting in small-data problems. 
On the other hand, machine learning models are well-suited for structured datasets with limited size, providing robust performance and greater interpretability. 
To evaluate classification performance, we tested a diverse set of algorithms: Logistic Regression, Random Forest, Support Vector Machines (SVM) with linear, radial 
basis function (RBF), and polynomial kernels, Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN). These models offer a mix of linear and non-linear 
decision boundaries, ensemble learning, and instance-based reasoning, enabling a comprehensive comparison across different learning paradigms.


All models were evaluated using fixed hyperparameters selected to balance bias–variance trade-offs in small-sample clinical datasets; the complete parameter 
settings are reported in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparameters} for reproducibility.

\subsection{Evaluation Strategy}
Given the limited size and the subject-level nature of the dataset, leave-one-out and stratified cross-validation were selected to maximize generalization across 
individuals while avoiding data leakage. External validation on independent cohorts remains an important next step and will be addressed in future work as additional 
datasets become available through ongoing clinical studies. All hyperparameter configurations were kept constant across cross-validation folds, as specified in 
Table~\ref{tab:hyperparameters}.
