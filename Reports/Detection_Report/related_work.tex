\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}
To contextualize our approach, we researched and reviewed studies focused on gait cycle analysis and classification, particularly in post-stroke or pathological gait 
conditions. Starting with the work of Yuwono et al \cite{ref20}, they proposed a method for classifying healthy and pathological gait using Shimmer IMUs placed on the 
legs, where they extracted spectral features from the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) of the gyroscope signals. Their method achieved a classification accuracy 
of 97\% using a Self-Organising Map (SOM) combined with k-means clustering. Following this, Zhou et al \cite{ref21} developed an adaptive gait event detection 
algorithm using a smart insole solution with pressure sensors and IMUs.

Further advancements were made by Tunca et al \cite{ref22}, that introduced a smartphone and smartwatch-based system that utilised raw IMU data without preprocessing,
using a deep learning model called DeepSense, which combined Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) and Gated Recurrent Units (GRU) with the aim of creating a 
general purpose framework for human activity recognition with a 94\% accuracy. Boudarham et al \cite{ref23} assessed gait variability in stroke patients using an 
8-camera motion capture system, focusing on sagittal joint angles and employing Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to reduce dimensionality, although they did not 
provide specific classification metrics. 

Mizuta et al \cite{ref34} used OpenPose to extract limb angles and lengths from video data, clustering post-stroke gait into interpretable patterns using Gaussian 
Mixture Models. Following this approach, Mohammed et al \cite{ref24} segmented gait phases from in-shoe pressure sensor data using regression and thresholding 
techniques, achieving qualitative results that matched expert assessments. Furthermore, Zhou et al \cite{ref25} improved single-IMU worn at the waist to get 
gait recognition by fusing temporal and spatial features using a CNN and Bi-LSTM, achieving an accuracy of 98.63\%.

More recently, Agostini et al \cite{ref26} used three switches per foot to create a dataset of gait phase strings, achieving 100\% accuracy for healthy subjects 
and 98\% for pathological cases using rule-based segmentation. Ferrante et al \cite{ref27} made use of 3D motion capture to assess the effect of biofeedback cycling on 
gait recovery, showing a 51\% improvement in gait patterns. Lastly Kaczmarczyk et al \cite{ref28} used an Artificial Neural Network (ANN) to classify post-stroke gait 
patterns from joint angles captured by the APAS motion capture system, achieving an accuracy of approximately 92.5\%.

These studies highlight the diversity of approaches in gait analysis, from traditional motion capture systems to wearable sensors and machine learning techniques.
Provided all of the studies, there is a notable lack of a lightweight and interpretable method specifically designed to detect clinically meaningful asymmetries in 
the limited data of a gait cycle, especially in populations recovering from neurological events such as stroke.
