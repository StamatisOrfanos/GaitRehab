\subsection{Data Source}
This study builds upon the dataset introduced by Sanghan et al.~\cite{ref22}, which compared 3D shank kinematics between sixteen stroke patients and sixteen 
healthy individuals. Each participant walked on a flat 10-meter indoor walkway while wearing IMU sensors (MBIENTLAB Inc., USA) bilaterally on the shanks. 
Gyroscope data were collected at 100 Hz across sagittal (x), frontal (y), and transverse (z) axes. This work focuses solely on the z-axis signal, which best 
captures sagittal-plane gait motion.

\subsection{Feature Engineering}
To assess gait asymmetry, we developed a lightweight pipeline that processes bilateral z-axis gyroscope signals to extract gait cycles and generate 
interpretable features.

\textbf{Gait Cycle Detection:}
We define $G_L(t)$ as the filtered z-axis gyroscope signal from the left shank. Gait cycles are identified by detecting local peaks:
\[
G_L(p_i) > \theta \quad \text{and} \quad p_{i+1} - p_i \geq d,
\]
where $\theta = 0.5$ deg/s and $d = 80$ samples (at 100 Hz). Each gait cycle spans the interval $[p_i, p_{i+1})$ and is retained only if its duration 
exceeds 10 samples.

\textbf{Feature Computation:}
For each valid cycle, we extract:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Descriptive statistics:} mean, standard deviation, min, and max of $G_L(t)$ and $G_R(t)$.
    \item \textit{Motion score:} 
    \[
    M = \max(|G(t)|) - \min(|G(t)|).
    \]
    \item \textit{Stance and swing phase durations:} derived via zero-crossing detection and computed as
    \[
    T_{\text{stance}} = t_{\min} - t_{\text{start}}, \quad
    T_{\text{swing}} = t_{\text{end}} - t_{\min},
    \]
    where $t_{\min}$ corresponds to the minimum angular velocity within each phase.
    \item \textit{Stride time:}
    \[
    T_{\text{stride}} = T_{\text{stance}} + T_{\text{swing}}.
    \]
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Asymmetry Metrics:}
We compute two bilateral stride-based asymmetry metrics:
\[
\text{AI} = \frac{T_L - T_R}{T_L + T_R}, \quad
\text{SR} = \frac{\min(T_L, T_R)}{\max(T_L, T_R)},
\]
where $T_L$ and $T_R$ are stride durations for the left and right shank respectively. Cycles with undefined values are marked as invalid. Out of 3721 total windows, 
2837 were retained as valid for analysis.

\newpage
\textbf{Confidence Level Assignment:}
Each gait cycle is assigned to one of three asymmetry confidence levels based on the AI and SR metrics:

\begin{itemize}
    \item High Confidence: $\text{AI} > 0.2$ or $\text{SR} < 0.8$
    \item Moderate Confidence: $\text{AI} > 0.15$ or $\text{SR} < 0.85$
    \item Low Confidence: $\text{AI} > 0.1$ or $\text{SR} < 0.9$
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Feature Selection}
To reduce dimensionality and enhance interpretability, we employed Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) using a Random Forest classifier. Features were 
iteratively ranked by importance until the optimal subset was identified. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:rfe}, stance and swing times for both legs, along 
with motion scores, were consistently selected across confidence levels.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Recursive Feature Elimination Results}
\label{tab:rfe}
    \begin{tabular}{lcc}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Feature} & \textbf{Importance} & \textbf{Selected} \\
    \midrule
        Right stance time   & 0.18 & True \\
        Right swing time    & 0.12 & True \\
        Left stance time    & 0.11 & True \\
        Left swing time     & 0.05 & True \\
        Left motion score   & 0.05 & True \\
        Right motion score  & 0.05 & True \\
    \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The stance and stride times were consistently ranked as the most important across all confidence levels, while the left motion score was selected for the high 
and moderate confidence levels, and the right motion score for the low confidence level. To preserve real-time applicability and medical relevance, we prioritized 
stride and stance times as our primary input features.

\subsection{Classification Models}
Given the structured nature and limited size of the dataset, we employed traditional machine learning models rather than deep learning approaches. 
The selected classifiers included: Logistic Regression, Random Forest, Support Vector Machines (SVM) with linear, RBF, and polynomial kernels, Extreme 
Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN). This ensemble covers a range of decision boundaries—from linear to non-parametric—enabling 
a robust comparison across feature complexity and asymmetry levels.
