
\section{Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:materials_and_methods}
\subsection{Data Source}
This study builds upon the dataset introduced by Sanghan et al.~\cite{ref23}, which compared 3D shank kinematics between sixteen stroke patients and sixteen 
healthy individuals. Each participant walked on a flat 10-meter indoor walkway while wearing IMU sensors (MBIENTLAB Inc., USA) bilaterally on the shanks. 
Gyroscope data were collected at 100 Hz across frontal (x), transverse (y), and sagittal (z) axes. This current work focuses solely on the z-axis signal, 
which best captures sagittal-plane gait motion.

\subsection{System Overview}
Figure~\ref{fig:system_architecture} illustrates the system architecture used in this study. IMUs were placed bilaterally on the 
lower shanks to capture sagittal-plane motion via z-axis gyroscope signals during gait movement. The raw sensor data were transmitted via Bluetooth to the
mobile application we developed, where we applied a data pipeline and fed it to a lightweight machine learning model that processes the data in real time,
providing immediate feedback on gait symmetry to the user through an interface and a vibration alert.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Images/architecture.png}
    \caption{System architecture showing IMU data acquisition, data pipeline, classification, and feedback delivery.}
    \label{fig:system_architecture}
\end{figure}

In alignment with the training pipeline, the mobile application applied the same preprocessing steps used during model development, including the 
application of an identical normalization scaler to ensure consistency in feature distributions. Predictions are generated every 2 seconds, a window 
size empirically chosen to capture at least one complete gait cycle, including both left and right leg movement. This ensured temporal resolution sufficient 
for robust asymmetry detection while minimizing computational overhead. One important goal was to enhance user experience and prevent over-alerting, the system 
incorporates a staggered vibration feedback mechanism that limits repetitive notifications. This design choice balances responsiveness with usability, ensuring that 
feedback is both timely and meaningful without overwhelming the user during prolonged walking sessions.

\subsection{Feature Engineering}
To assess gait asymmetry, we developed a lightweight pipeline that processes bilateral z-axis gyroscope signals to extract gait cycles and generate 
interpretable features.

\textbf{Gait Cycle Detection:}
We define $G_L(t)$ as the filtered z-axis gyroscope signal from the left shank. Gait cycles are identified by detecting local peaks:
\[
G_L(p_i) > \theta \quad \text{and} \quad p_{i+1} - p_i \geq d,
\]
where $\theta = 0.5$ deg/s and $d = 80$ samples (at 100 Hz). Each gait cycle spans the interval $[p_i, p_{i+1})$ and is retained only if its duration 
exceeds 10 samples.

\textbf{Feature Computation:}
For each valid cycle, we extracted:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Descriptive statistics:} mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum of $G_L(t)$ and $G_R(t)$.
    \item \textit{Motion score:}
    \[
    M = \max(|G(t)|) - \min(|G(t)|).
    \]
    \item \textit{Stance and swing phase durations:} derived via zero-crossing detection and computed as
    \[
    T_{\text{stance}} = t_{\min} - t_{\text{start}}, \quad
    T_{\text{swing}} = t_{\text{end}} - t_{\min},
    \]
    where $t_{\min}$ corresponds to the time of the minimum angular velocity within each phase.
    \item \textit{Stride time:}
    \[
    T_{\text{stride}} = T_{\text{stance}} + T_{\text{swing}}.
    \]
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Asymmetry Metrics:}
We also computed two bilateral stride-based asymmetry metrics Asymmetry Index (AI) and Symmetry Ratio (SR) to quantify gait asymmetry:
\[
\text{AI} = \frac{T_L - T_R}{T_L + T_R}, \quad
\text{SR} = \frac{\min(T_L, T_R)}{\max(T_L, T_R)},
\]
where $T_L$ and $T_R$ are stride durations for the left and right shank respectively. Cycles with undefined values are marked as invalid. Out of 3,721 total windows, 
2,837 were retained as valid for analysis.

\newpage
\textbf{Confidence Level Assignment:}
Defining multiple levels of confidence allowed us to stratify gait cycles by the degree of asymmetry, enabling more nuanced model evaluation and ensuring that 
the classification system can adapt its sensitivity based on clinical risk or application context. Each gait cycle is assigned to one of three asymmetry confidence 
levels based on the AI and SR metrics:

\begin{itemize}
    \item High Confidence: $\text{AI} > 0.2$ or $\text{SR} < 0.8$
    \item Moderate Confidence: $\text{AI} > 0.15$ or $\text{SR} < 0.85$
    \item Low Confidence: $\text{AI} > 0.1$ or $\text{SR} < 0.9$
\end{itemize}

Table~\ref{tab:confidence_distribution} summarizes the class distribution by confidence level. While healthy gait windows dominate, stroke-related asymmetry 
is consistently represented across all tiers.

\subsection{Feature Selection}
In order to enhance interpretability, we employed Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) using a Random Forest classifier. Features were 
iteratively ranked by importance until the optimal subset was identified as shown in Table~\ref{tab:rfe}.

\begin{table}[!h]
\centering
\caption{Recursive Feature Elimination Results}
\label{tab:rfe}
    \begin{tabular}{lcc}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Feature} & \textbf{Importance} & \textbf{Selected} \\
    \midrule
        Right stance time   & 0.18 & True \\
        Right swing time    & 0.12 & True \\
        Left stance time    & 0.11 & True \\
        Left swing time     & 0.05 & True \\
        Left motion score   & 0.05 & True \\
        Right motion score  & 0.05 & True \\
    \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The stance and stride times were consistently ranked as the most important across all confidence levels, while the left motion score was selected for the high 
and moderate confidence levels, and the right motion score for the low confidence level. To preserve real-time applicability and medical relevance, we prioritized 
stride and stance times as our primary input features.
